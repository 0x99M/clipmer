Most productivity advice for Ubuntu is a list of apps to install. This one is mostly
keyboard habits that are already on your machine, each with an honest estimate of the time
it saves. Added up, the nine below come to **about 25 hours a year** for someone at a
desk all day. The working comes first, so you can swap in your own numbers.

Every shortcut here is a default on Ubuntu 26.04 LTS with GNOME 50, read from the system's
own settings schemas. Most are unchanged on 24.04.

## The estimate, and how it was made

Each habit gets three numbers: how often a typical desk worker does the thing, how many
seconds the habit saves each time compared with the mouse route, and the product of the
two. A working year is taken as **230 days**: 260 weekdays, minus holidays and leave.

These are estimates, not measurements. Nobody timed a panel of users; the seconds are what
the mouse route costs when you do it at a normal pace, and the counts are deliberately
middling. If you live in two apps all day, habit 3 is worth more and habit 1 less. The
point of writing the numbers down is that you can disagree with a specific one.

## 1. Launch apps by typing

Press **Super**, type the first letters of the app, press **Enter**. The overview searches
apps, settings panels and files as you type, so `term` finds Terminal and `blu` finds the
Bluetooth settings panel. Reaching for the app grid with **Super + A** and scrolling is the slow
route this replaces.

## 2. Super + 1–9 for the apps in your dock

The Ubuntu Dock numbers its pinned apps from the top. **Super + 1** opens or focuses the
first, **Super + 2** the second, and so on. Pin the five apps you use most, in a fixed
order, and you stop looking for them.

## 3. Alt + ` between windows of the same app

**Alt + Tab** switches between *applications*. **Alt + `** — the key above Tab — switches
between windows *of the current application*: two browser windows, three terminals, the
document and its export. It's the shortcut people most often don't know exists, and the
one that replaces the most Alt+Tab-then-squint.

## 4. Super + ← and Super + → to tile

**Super + ←** puts the focused window on the left half of the screen, **Super + →** on the
right. A reference on one side and the thing you are writing on the other takes two key
presses instead of two drags and two resizes. Press the same key again to restore the
window.

## 5. Workspaces from the keyboard

**Super + Page Down** and **Super + Page Up** move to the next and previous workspace;
**Ctrl + Alt + → / ←** do the same. Add **Shift** to take the focused window with you.
One workspace for communication and one for the work itself means the chat window stops
covering the editor, and switching back is a key press rather than minimising three things.

## 6. Print for screenshots

**Print** opens GNOME's screenshot tool with an area already selectable. **Shift + Print**
captures the whole screen and **Alt + Print** the focused window, both without any dialog.
Every screenshot is saved to `~/Pictures/Screenshots` *and* put on the clipboard, so you
can paste it straight into a chat or a bug report. **Ctrl + Shift + Alt + R** starts a
screen recording.

## 7. Ctrl + L in Files

In the Files app, **Ctrl + L** turns the breadcrumb bar into a text field. Type or paste
a path and press Enter. Typing `/` or `~` does the same. For a folder six levels deep that
you already know the path to, it beats clicking through every level.

## 8. One custom shortcut for the thing you open every day

**Settings → Keyboard → View and Customize Shortcuts → Custom Shortcuts** lets you bind
any command to a key. Give one to the script, folder or app you open several times a day
and has no shortcut of its own: `nautilus ~/Projects/current`, a VPN toggle, a notes file.
One is enough to start; most people who add one add three more within a month.

**Ctrl + Alt + T**, which opens a terminal, is the built-in example of the same idea.

## 9. A clipboard history

This is the biggest number in the table, and the only habit that needs something
installed: Ubuntu keeps one clipboard item, and the next copy replaces it. Two things go
wrong because of that, several times a day:

- **You need something you copied earlier.** A link from an hour ago, a value you copied
  before the one you just pasted. Without a history, you go back to wherever it came from
  and find it again. Four times a day at about 30 seconds is two minutes.
- **You retype things you've copied many times.** An address, a command, a standard
  reply. Five times a day at 15 seconds is another minute and a quarter.

A clipboard history keeps every copy, and searching it takes a few letters.

## Building the habits

A shortcut saves nothing until you stop reaching for the mouse, and that takes about a
week per habit. Two things make it stick:

- **One at a time.** Pick the habit that matches the thing you do most, use only that one
  for a week, then add the next. Learning nine at once means learning none.
- **Catch yourself, don't plan.** The moment you notice your hand on the way to the mouse
  for one of these, stop and press the keys instead. It feels slower for two days and then
  it isn't.
