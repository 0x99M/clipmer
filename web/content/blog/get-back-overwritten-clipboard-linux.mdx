You copied something, then copied something else, and the first thing is gone. On most
Linux desktops that is exactly what happened: the clipboard holds one item, and the moment
a new copy lands the old one stops existing anywhere the clipboard can reach. There is no
undo for it and no hidden history to dig through.

There are a few places the text might still be, though, and one of them surprises most
people. Check them in this order, because the first two only last until you select or
close something else.

## The short answer

- **Highlighted it with the mouse?** Middle-click into any text field. The primary
  selection is separate from Ctrl+C, and it may still hold it.
- **Is the app you copied from still open?** Go back to it. The text is usually still
  sitting where you copied it from, or one Ctrl+Z away.
- **Was it a command?** Your shell history has it.
- **On KDE Plasma?** Press **Meta + V**. Plasma keeps a clipboard history by default.
- **On GNOME, Sway, Hyprland or anything else:** nothing kept it. The rest of this page is
  about making sure that is the last time.

## Check the primary selection first

Linux has two clipboards, and most people only ever think about one of them. **Ctrl+C**
writes the *clipboard*. Merely highlighting text writes the *primary selection*, which is
what a middle-click pastes. They are independent: copying over one does not touch the
other.

That gives you a real chance in one common case. If the thing you lost was highlighted
and copied, and what replaced it came from a **Copy button** — a code block on a web page,
a "copy link" menu item, a password manager — then the replacement never touched the
primary selection. The text you lost is still there. Click into an empty text field and
press the middle mouse button. On a touchpad, a three-finger tap usually does the same.

It stops working the moment you highlight anything else, because every highlight
overwrites the primary selection. So check it before you do anything else, including
selecting text on this page.

<Callout type="note" title="It works on Wayland too">
  The primary selection is not an X11 leftover. GNOME, KDE Plasma and the wlroots
  compositors all support it on Wayland, and middle-click paste is on by default in GNOME.
</Callout>

## Go back to where you copied it from

Obvious, but it is the one people skip in a panic. If the source is still open, the text
almost always still exists there. If you had *cut* rather than copied, the editor's undo
puts it back: Ctrl+Z in the place you cut from restores the text, and the clipboard
doesn't matter.

A few other places keep their own copy without being asked:

- **Commands:** your shell history. `history | grep ssh`, or **Ctrl+R** and a few letters
  of the command, searches it.
- **URLs:** your browser history.
- **Terminal output:** the terminal's scrollback, until you close the tab.

## On KDE Plasma, you already have a history

Plasma is the one major Linux desktop that ships a clipboard history switched on. It is the
**Clipboard** icon in the system tray, and **Meta + V** opens it at the mouse pointer.
Choose the entry you lost and it goes back on the clipboard.

If it is empty, or only ever shows one item, something is misconfigured. Our
[guide to the KDE clipboard](/blog/kde-plasma-clipboard-history-not-working) covers the
usual causes.

## On GNOME and everything else, nothing kept it

GNOME has no clipboard history, and neither do Sway, Hyprland, niri or the other
wlroots compositors. Pressing **Super + V** on Ubuntu opens the notification list, not a
clipboard. Once a copy is overwritten on these desktops, no part of the system holds the
old one, and no recovery tool can get back data that was never stored.

That is the honest answer, and it is also the reason clipboard managers exist. A clipboard
manager doesn't recover anything; it records each copy as you make it, so the next time
there is something to go back to.

## Why Linux does not keep it for you

On both X11 and Wayland, copying doesn't put your text anywhere central. The application
you copied from announces that it *has* something, and only hands it over when another
application asks to paste. That keeps a 40 MB copy from being duplicated on every
Ctrl+C. It also means there is no system-wide store to look in afterwards. When a new copy
arrives, the old owner simply stops being asked.

The same design is why copied text can vanish when you close the app it came from, which
is a different problem with a different fix. [Why your clipboard empties when you close an
app](/blog/clipboard-empty-after-closing-app-linux) covers which desktops guard against
that, and which leave it to you.
