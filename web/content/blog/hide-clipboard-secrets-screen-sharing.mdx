It goes like this. You're sharing your screen on a call, someone asks for the staging URL,
and you open your clipboard history to find it. For two seconds, everyone on the call sees
everything you have copied today: the database password from this morning, a personal
access token, a customer's email address, the SSH command for a box that isn't supposed to
exist.

Nobody screenshots it, probably. But a recorded call keeps it, and you can't take it back.
This isn't a Linux problem; it comes with any clipboard history. When Apple added one to
Spotlight in macOS Tahoe, the very next update, 26.1, added a switch to turn it off, a
button to clear it and an expiry timer, and
[Macworld's write-up](https://www.macworld.com/article/2962021/this-macos-tahoe-26-1-setting-will-eliminate-embarrassing-clipboard-mishaps.html)
framed all three as answers to a privacy risk.

Turning it off is one answer. This page is about the other one: keeping the history and
choosing what it shows.

## Why a clipboard history is the worst thing to have on screen

Most of what you share on a call is something you chose to share: a document, a terminal,
a browser tab. A clipboard history is the opposite. It's a list of everything you moved
around today, including the things you moved precisely *because* they were too sensitive
to type — passwords pasted from a password manager, keys pasted into a config file, tokens
pasted into a header.

It's also the window you open on reflex, in a hurry, while someone is waiting. That
combination is why it leaks.

## Habits that help with any tool

None of these need a particular clipboard manager:

- **Share a window, not your screen.** GNOME, KDE Plasma and every browser-based meeting
  tool let you pick a single window. A clipboard popup that opens over a different window
  isn't captured at all.
- **Clear the history before a planned demo.** Most clipboard managers have a clear button;
  remember what it keeps (see below).
- **Treat a leak as a leak.** If a token or password was visible on a recorded call,
  rotate it. Blurring the recording afterwards doesn't un-share the live stream.

## Hiding individual entries

Clearing everything before every call throws away the useful history along with the
dangerous part. What you actually want is for the dangerous entries to stop showing,
while the rest stays.

the feature is **free**:

Open the history with **Ctrl + Shift + D**, open an entry's **⋯** menu, and choose
**Hide**. From then on that entry:

- **Shows as a fixed row of dots in the list.** Always the same ten dots, so the length of
  the secret doesn't leak either.
- **Is left out of search.** Typing `pass` or `token` while sharing can't bring it back up
  as a result.
- **Opens masked in the full-content viewer.** The viewer says *Masked* and shows nothing
  until you press **Reveal**, and it withholds the character and line counts while masked —
  the shape of a secret is often enough to identify it.
- **Still pastes normally.** Choose it and its real contents go on the clipboard. Hiding
  changes what's on screen, not what the entry does.

Choose **Unhide** from the same menu to reverse it.

## The window gets out of the way

Two smaller things matter more than they sound:

- **Clipmer's window hides as soon as it loses focus.** Click back into your meeting or
  your editor and it's gone, rather than staying pinned on top of whatever you are
  sharing.
- **The viewer and the ⋯ menu close with it.** Re-open the window mid-call and you get the
  list, not whatever entry you had open last time.

## What clearing keeps

**Clear history** in Settings, and **Clear Unsaved History** in the tray menu, wipe
everything except entries you have filed in folders. Folder entries are the ones you chose
to keep, so they survive on purpose — which means a password you once filed in a folder
survives too. Hide it, or delete it individually from its ⋯ menu. Folders are also how a
snippet outlives the entry limit —
[using clipboard history as a snippet library](/blog/reuse-text-snippets-linux#using-clipboard-history-as-a-snippet-library)
covers that side of them.

Clearing also doesn't clear the system clipboard. Whatever you copied last is still what
Ctrl+V pastes; [clearing the clipboard on Linux](/blog/clear-clipboard-linux) covers
emptying that too.

## What hiding does not do

Being clear about the limits is the point of a feature like this:

- **It isn't encryption.** A hidden entry is stored exactly like any other, in plain text in
  `~/.config/clipmer/clipmer-data.json`. Hiding protects against someone *watching your
  screen*, not someone with access to your files.
- **It isn't automatic.** You hide entries yourself. Password managers mark the passwords
  they copy with a hint (`x-kde-passwordManagerHint`), and some clipboard managers, Klipper
  and CopyQ among them, use it to skip those entries entirely. Clipmer doesn't read that
  hint yet. [Clipboard managers that save passwords](/blog/clipboard-manager-saving-passwords-linux)
  goes through which ones do.
- **It doesn't stop you pasting.** If you press Ctrl+V in the window you're sharing, the
  secret appears there, hidden entry or not.

We audited this feature ourselves after shipping it, found that the full-content viewer
used to bypass it entirely, and wrote up [what we found](/blog/auditing-our-own-clipboard-manager).

## If you don't want a history at all

That's a reasonable choice, and switching it off — what macOS 26.1 now offers — is the
honest version of it. You lose the ability to get back the thing you copied five minutes ago,
which is the reason people install a clipboard manager in the first place. If you'd rather
keep that, [what you can still get back without one](/blog/get-back-overwritten-clipboard-linux)
is shorter than most people hope.
